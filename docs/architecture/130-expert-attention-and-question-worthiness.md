# Expert Attention and Question Worthiness

## 1. Purpose

High-quality expert interaction is not produced
by asking the largest possible number of questions.

Excellent human experts often notice something subtle
and naturally stop:

> "Something here matters. I should clarify this."

SPA-based systems should model this expert attention.

The goal is not:

> Ask everything the AI does not know.

The goal is:

> Notice what an excellent human expert would find
> consequential enough to stop and clarify.

A concise principle is:

> Ask less. Notice better.

---

## 2. Expert Attention

Expertise includes knowing where to direct attention.

An expert does not merely possess domain knowledge.
An expert notices:

- distinctions that may otherwise be flattened,
- tensions that may not be true contradictions,
- moments where timing changes meaning,
- unknowns that materially affect the outcome,
- and interpretations that may distort the human source.

This moment of professional attention can be understood as:

> "Wait — what exactly is happening here?"

This is not failure to understand.

It is disciplined recognition that clarification
may protect meaning or substantially improve the result.

---

## 3. From Unknown Detection to Expert Attention

A simple clarification system may behave like this:

Unknown

→ Ask question

→ Fill field

This tends to produce excessive questioning
and encourages artificial completeness.

Expert Attention uses a different flow:

Human Expression

→ Expert Attention

→ Semantic Tension Detection

→ Question Worthiness

→ Natural Clarification

→ Human Confirmation

→ Confirmed Semantic State

→ Expert Translation

Unknown alone is not sufficient reason to ask.

The system should ask when clarification is consequential.

---

## 4. Initial Attention Categories

The following categories provide an initial model
for expert attention.

### 4.1 Semantic Tension

Two statements appear different or potentially conflicting.

The system must not immediately assume
that the newer statement replaces the earlier statement.

It should consider whether both may be true.

Question:

> Is this a contradiction, refinement,
> different layer, or different moment?

---

### 4.2 Layer Difference

Two descriptions may refer to different semantic layers.

Examples include:

- baseline state,
- momentary expression,
- remembered impression,
- interpretation,
- intention,
- observable fact.

Flattening these layers may preserve words
while destroying meaning.

The system should notice when apparently competing descriptions
may belong to different layers.

---

### 4.3 Temporal Significance

Meaning may depend on when something occurred.

The system should distinguish when material:

Baseline

→ Trigger

→ Emergence

→ Aftermath

A state that existed before an event
must not automatically replace
an expression that emerged at the decisive moment.

---

### 4.4 Decisive Unknown

Not every unknown deserves clarification.

A Decisive Unknown is an unknown
whose resolution could materially change:

- meaning,
- professional judgment,
- representation,
- safety,
- or the quality of the final output.

The system should prioritize these unknowns
and preserve non-decisive unknowns without forcing completion.

---

### 4.5 Representation Risk

Sometimes the semantic record appears sufficient,
but translating it into an output may introduce distortion.

The expert layer should notice:

> If I proceed with this interpretation,
> could the resulting representation communicate
> something the human did not mean?

When representation risk is high,
clarification may be necessary before expert translation.

---

## 5. Question Worthiness

Expert Attention does not automatically produce a question.

After noticing something,
the system should evaluate whether asking is worthwhile.

A clarification question has high Question Worthiness when:

- the ambiguity is semantically important,
- different answers would materially change the output,
- proceeding without confirmation risks distortion,
- the human can reasonably answer,
- and the question cannot be safely resolved
  from already confirmed information.

A question has low Question Worthiness when:

- it merely fills an optional field,
- it concerns decorative detail,
- the answer would not materially affect the result,
- the information was never observed or remembered,
- or preserving Unknown is sufficient.

---

## 6. Natural Clarification

When clarification is worthwhile,
the system should ask naturally.

Questions should not feel like
a database form or exhaustive questionnaire.

Where appropriate, the system may provide:

- a small number of meaningful choices,
- an explicit Unknown / not remembered option,
- and a free-expression option.

Choices must help the human distinguish meaning.

They must not pressure the human
to adopt AI-generated meaning.

---

## 7. Visual Testimony Example

Consider testimony containing:

Earlier statement:

> "Her expression showed surprise and joy."

Later statement:

> "There was tenderness."

A weak system may replace the earlier expression
with the later one.

An Expert Attention system notices:

> These may not be competing descriptions.
> Are they describing different layers or moments?

Clarification may reveal:

Baseline:
tenderness

Trigger:
recognition of the loved person

Emergent expression:
surprise and joy

The important achievement is not
that the AI guessed this structure correctly.

The achievement is that the AI noticed
the distinction was important enough
to let the witness clarify it.

---

## 8. Expert Freedom and Semantic Restraint

After important meaning has been clarified
and confirmed, the expert layer may exercise
high professional capability.

In Visual Testimony,
this may resemble an excellent photographer
deciding:

- which instant matters,
- where to stand,
- what to emphasize,
- and how to make the witnessed truth visible.

The expert may be highly creative
about professional expression.

The expert must remain conservative
about human semantic truth.

---

## 9. Cross-Domain Application

Expert Attention is not specific
to image generation.

Different domains provide different expert attention models.

### Visual Testimony

An excellent photographer or interviewer may notice:

- emotional layers,
- decisive moments,
- temporal transitions,
- visual ambiguity,
- or representation risk.

### Care / AIKO

An excellent service management professional may notice:

- a difference between the person's wish
  and a supporter's interpretation,
- an unsupported assumption about difficulty,
- tension between goals and proposed support,
- or a missing fact that would materially change support design.

### Other Domains

Research, education, design, planning,
and other professional domains may define
their own attention patterns.

The shared architecture remains:

Human Meaning

→ Expert Attention

→ Question Worthiness

→ Human Clarification

→ Confirmed Meaning

→ Expert Translation

---

## 10. Quality Principle

The quality of an expert clarification system
should not be measured by the number of questions asked.

A better system:

- interrupts less,
- notices more,
- asks at consequential moments,
- preserves Unknown when appropriate,
- and improves expert output without taking semantic authority
  away from the human.

Therefore:

> Expert AI should not ask everything it does not know.
>
> It should notice what an excellent human expert
> would find consequential enough to stop and clarify.

And:

> Ask less. Notice better.
